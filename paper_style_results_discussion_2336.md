# Results and Discussion

To characterize the conformational landscape of the protein-protein complex under AWH sampling, we analyzed the four-walker ensemble after excluding the first 400 ns of simulation. This cutoff reduced the contribution of early transient behavior and retained a substantial post-transient dataset for structural and state-space analysis. Dimensionality reduction in TICA space revealed a heterogeneous free-energy landscape comprising one dominant basin and at least three additional metastable regions. The dominant macrostate accounted for approximately 62.6% of the analyzed ensemble, whereas the remaining macrostates contributed 18.5%, 13.3%, and 5.7%, respectively. These data indicate that the bound ensemble is not represented by a single rigid configuration, but rather by multiple conformational substates and intermediates.

The walker-resolved projections further showed that sampling overlap among the four walkers remained incomplete. Walkers 0 and 1 were almost entirely confined to the dominant basin, walker 2 mainly sampled the dominant and one adjacent basin, whereas walker 3 preferentially explored two alternative regions of conformational space. UMAP provided an even clearer separation of these regions, supporting the presence of distinct metastable substates rather than simple fluctuations within one basin. Therefore, the 400 ns cutoff appears sufficient for qualitative mapping of the post-transient landscape, but the incomplete overlap between walkers suggests that the ensemble should not yet be interpreted as fully converged.

MSM analysis supported the existence of multiple metastable states, but also highlighted limitations in kinetic interpretation. The implied timescales increased steadily with lag time and did not show a clear plateau within the scanned range, indicating that the current model is more reliable for qualitative state decomposition than for extracting converged absolute kinetics. Accordingly, the MSM network is best interpreted as a representation of relative state connectivity and pathway organization rather than a definitive kinetic model. In the context of biased AWH sampling, this distinction is particularly important, because state populations and transition times may still reflect the influence of the biasing protocol.

Interface descriptors were consistent with a structurally heterogeneous binding process. The average number of intermolecular hydrogen bonds differed markedly among walkers, with mean values of 1.41, 1.06, 4.11, and 9.83 for walkers 0 to 3, respectively. This large variation indicates that different segments of the sampled landscape correspond to substantially different degrees of interface stabilization. In parallel, the average contact map revealed a limited number of pronounced contact hotspots rather than a broad, uniformly packed binding surface, suggesting that the complex is stabilized by selective interfacial patches. Together, these observations support a model in which multiple interface arrangements coexist and contribute differently to complex stability along the AWH-sampled coordinate.

Global structural descriptors further emphasized the flexible nature of the analyzed ensemble. RMSD remained broadly distributed throughout the analyzed interval rather than converging tightly to a single structural cluster, while RMSF profiles indicated enhanced mobility near the termini and appreciable fluctuations across the central region as well. DSSP analysis showed that the analyzed chain was dominated by coil (76.3%) and bend (17.3%) states, whereas persistent helix and strand occupancies remained very low. This pattern is consistent with a conformationally adaptable binding partner that can reorganize its local structure while exploring alternative interface geometries.

Overall, the post-400 ns AWH ensemble supports a mechanistic picture in which binding and partial unbinding occur through a diverse set of conformational substates rather than through a single well-defined structural transition. The present data are therefore well suited for discussing conformational heterogeneity, interface plasticity, and pathway diversity. However, because walker mixing remains incomplete and MSM implied timescales are not yet fully converged, quantitative conclusions regarding equilibrium populations or absolute kinetics should be stated cautiously. In its current form, this dataset provides strong support for qualitative mechanistic interpretation, while additional sampling or convergence testing would be beneficial for a more rigorous thermodynamic or kinetic analysis.

# Shorter Version

After excluding the first 400 ns, the four-walker AWH ensemble revealed a heterogeneous conformational landscape with one dominant basin and several additional metastable regions in TICA space. This result indicates that the protein-protein complex does not populate a single rigid bound state, but instead samples multiple substates and intermediates. Walker-resolved projections showed incomplete overlap among the four walkers, suggesting that the chosen cutoff is sufficient for qualitative state-space analysis but not yet sufficient to establish full ensemble convergence.

Interface analysis supported this interpretation. The number of intermolecular hydrogen bonds differed strongly among walkers, and the average contact map highlighted only a few pronounced interfacial hotspots, indicating that different parts of the landscape correspond to distinct modes of interface stabilization. In addition, RMSD, RMSF, and DSSP analyses showed that the system remains structurally flexible, with a strong predominance of coil and bend states. Taken together, these results support a model of binding and partial unbinding through multiple flexible interface arrangements. Nevertheless, because the implied timescales do not show a clear plateau and walker mixing remains incomplete, MSM-derived kinetics and equilibrium populations should be interpreted with caution.
